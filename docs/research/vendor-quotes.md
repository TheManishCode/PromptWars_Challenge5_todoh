# Research study: collecting vendor data and quotes with an AI-assisted planner (2026-11-06)

## How this was done, and what it does not show
- Searched arXiv (its API) and OpenAlex for the problems below, then read the **abstracts** of the papers cited. Full texts were not read. Numbers quoted are from abstracts.
- Read in full the part of Google's Places API policy page that governs storing and showing Places content.
- **No paper was found on collecting event-vendor quotes** (searches for request-for-quotation + LLM, wedding/event vendors + LLM or recommender, and vendor quote extraction returned nothing relevant). Everything below is by analogy from neighbouring work: LLM planning, agent marketplaces, information extraction, human-in-the-loop, entity resolution, reputation, procurement auctions.
- Most 2025-26 items are arXiv preprints, not peer reviewed, and most agent-market results are from simulated markets.

## Findings, and what each means for us

| # | Problem | Evidence (abstract-level) | Resolution for this system |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Can a model plan under many constraints? | TravelPlanner (arXiv 2402.01622): GPT-4 reaches a 0.6% success rate; agents lose track of multiple constraints. Chen et al. (2408.06318): models fail to attend to crucial parts of long context and cannot give accurate feedback on long plans, so self-refinement is unreliable. | Keep the solver + independent validator as the only thing that decides a plan. Quote-derived facts must pass the validator. Do not add a model that "picks the best vendor". |
| 2 | Is a schema-valid extraction a correct one? | Chavan (2609.23742): constrained decoding takes schema validity to 100% but a semantic gap remains: "schema conformance is necessary but not sufficient". | A strict schema (we have one) is not the human check. Keep the user confirmation for anything that affects money, diets or dates. |
| 3 | Can model confidence replace review? | Qian (2610.20417): confidence ranks but does not verify; one misattribution sentence flips high-confidence judgments in 95.6-100% of cases; "confidence tiering may set review priorities, never truth". Goh & Mueller CONSTRUCT (2603.18014): per-field trust scores used to prioritise limited human review. Zhang et al. (2601.15778): agents are overconfident when they fail. | If a model ever reads replies, its confidence only orders the review queue. Our rule of sending a reply with several amounts (price beside a deposit) to the user is the right one: this is the failure Qian measured. Show the vendor's source sentence beside every extracted field. |
| 4 | Vendor text is untrusted input for a model | Greshake et al. (2302.12173): indirect prompt injection through retrieved data. InjecAgent (2403.02691): ReAct-prompted GPT-4 vulnerable 24% of the time. Hashmi et al. (2609.30657): email-agent injection detectors reach a mean F1 of 0.406 on a strict threshold. | Keep vendor replies out of every prompt (the reader is rule-based). If a model must read them: extraction only, no tools, output validated against the schema, then confirmed by the user. |
| 5 | Agents choose the first responder | Magentic Marketplace (2510.25779): all models show "severe first-proposal bias, creating 10-30x advantages for response speed over quality"; performance degrades with scale. | The comparison must be order-blind: collect until a deadline or enough replies, then rank. Never auto-pick the first vendor to answer. |
| 6 | Counterparties overpromise | BazaarBench (2610.06748): under adversarial instructions, completed transactions despite unavailable items or overstated conditions rise from 15.4% to 33.4%. (LLM sellers, simulated.) | Treat "available" as a claim. A booking stays user-confirmed and needs the vendor's written confirmation. Expect vendors to adopt auto-replies. |
| 7 | Governing delegation | GAIA (2511.06262): authorization boundaries with escalation, information-gated progression (screening before commitment), a task-completeness index, human corrections as feedback. | Our approvals are the authorization boundary. Add a quote completeness index and gate comparison on it: a vendor with missing fields is "screening", not "comparable". Escalate to the user when a vendor asks for a deposit or changes terms. |
| 8 | Comparing price and quality | Che (RAND J. Econ. 1993, doi 10.2307/2555752): buyers score offers on price and quality with a scoring rule; the rule's design changes outcomes. | Fix the scoring rule (price, completeness, rating weights) before quotes are opened, show the formula, and do not publish it to vendors. |
| 9 | Ratings are weak evidence | Luca & Zervas (Management Science 2016): about 16% of Yelp restaurant reviews are filtered as suspicious; fraud is likelier when a business has few reviews. Tadelis (Annu. Rev. Econ. 2016): feedback systems carry bias. | Use rating only as a weak prior. Show the review count beside it. A 5.0 from 12 reviews must not outrank 4.8 from 581 (our shrinkage helps but cannot detect fraud). Quotes and replies are stronger evidence. |
| 10 | Same business, several records | Christophides et al. (ACM Comput. Surv. 2020): entity-resolution survey. Li et al. (2401.03426): ask a model only the most valuable matching pairs and tolerate its errors, to cut cost. Wijegunarathna et al. (2508.06584): LLMs are competitive on geospatial entity resolution. | De-duplicate listing, website and reply sender by place id, then name, address and phone. Use a model only for ambiguous pairs, and never merge silently (our "never merged" rule for discovered businesses agrees). |
| 11 | Conflicting or old facts | Li et al. truth-discovery survey (SIGKDD Explor. 2016); Ben Shabat et al. (2206.04816): weigh sources by estimated reliability; an empirical-Bayes step reduces error. | Keep provenance and date per field. Rank sources: vendor-confirmed quote > vendor's own site > listing. Show conflicting values with dates instead of averaging. Use shrinkage across vendors to give a clearly labelled price band while quotes are awaited. |
| 12 | When to ask the human | Madras et al. (1711.06664): learning to defer. Geifman & El-Yaniv (1705.08500): reject to meet a chosen risk level, trading coverage. Zhang et al. (2604.00904): human accuracy falls with workload. Bansal et al. (CHI 2021): explanations raised acceptance whatever the AI's correctness. | Ask only about fields that are high-consequence or low-confidence, and cap questions per session. Batch the approval of near-identical vendor emails (we ask one by one now). Show evidence, not persuasion, and require an active edit for risky fields. |
| 13 | Models guess instead of asking | Su & Cardie (2605.25284): models recognise ambiguity but "overwhelmingly default to direct answers". Zhang et al. (2410.13788): models can be trained to ask. | Keep the rule that ambiguity is an error and a question (our intake). If a model fills fields, rules still decide when to ask. |
| 14 | Vendors' real channel in India | De (2502.10913): small Indian businesses found WhatsApp Business complex and costly and adapted creatively. | Assume personal WhatsApp and calls, not an API. Offer click-to-chat with the request pre-filled for the user to send, and a short fixed reply template. Voice-note replies need transcription (the app already has audio code). |
| 15 | Evaluating the pipeline | UpBench (2511.12306): rubric-based evaluation with expert-written, verifiable acceptance criteria. | Build a seeded set of replies (clear, messy, wrong, late, with deposits and several amounts) with a rubric each. Our quote-reading random tests are the start. |
| 16 | May we store listing data? | Google Places policy page: "You must not pre-fetch, cache, or store Places API content beyond the allowed exceptions, although the place_id is exempt"; when showing Places data without a Google map the Google logo and attributions are required. | Store only `place_id` from Places. Re-fetch for display with attribution. Keep what the user confirmed (an address, a quote) as the user's own record. |

## What to build, in order
1. Fix storage and attribution of Places data (finding 16).
2. Vendor cards: everything held per vendor, each field with its source and date, plus direct-contact actions (findings 9, 10, 11, 14).
3. Fixed reply template and a completeness index per quote; amounts confirmed by the user (findings 2, 3, 7).
4. Order-blind comparison with a scoring rule fixed in advance (findings 5, 8).
5. Promote a confirmed quote to a plannable record, checked by the validator (findings 1, 6).
6. Batch approval, question budget, evidence-first review copy (finding 12).
7. Seeded reply set and rubric (finding 15).
